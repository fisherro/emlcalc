A scientific calculator goes well beyond the four basic operations. Here's what you'd normally expect on one:

**Basic and arithmetic**
- +, −, ×, ÷, with correct order of operations and parentheses
- Negation (±), percent, reciprocal (1/x)
- Memory registers (M+, M−, MR, MC, often several named variables) and an "Ans" recall of the last result
- Scientific and engineering notation (EXP/EE key), plus a choice of how many decimal places to show

**Powers, roots and logarithms**
- x², x³, xʸ, and 10ˣ and eˣ
- √x, ∛x, and the general ʸ√x
- log₁₀ and ln, sometimes log to any base

**Trigonometry**
- sin, cos, tan and their inverses
- Hyperbolic functions (sinh, cosh, tanh and their inverses)
- Degree, radian and gradian modes, plus conversion between them
- Degrees-minutes-seconds entry and conversion

**Constants**
- π and e
- Higher-end models add a table of physical constants (c, h, g, Avogadro's number and so on)

**Combinatorics and number functions**
- Factorial (n!), permutations (nPr), combinations (nCr)
- Absolute value, integer and fractional part, rounding
- Fractions and mixed numbers, with conversion between fraction and decimal
- Random numbers (a random decimal, and often a random integer in a range)
- Sometimes GCD/LCM and prime factorization

**Statistics**
- Entering one- or two-variable data
- Mean, sum, Σx², and sample and population standard deviation
- Linear regression (and often others: quadratic, log, exponential, power), the correlation coefficient, and predicted values

**Common on mid-range models**
- Polar ↔ rectangular coordinate conversion
- Complex number arithmetic
- Base-n mode: hex, decimal, octal, binary, with bitwise AND/OR/XOR/NOT
- Solving equations (quadratic and cubic, systems of linear equations, a numeric solver)
- Numeric integration and differentiation at a point
- Matrix and vector operations
- Unit conversions
- A multi-line display that shows the expression in textbook-style math notation, with history you can scroll back through and edit

Graphing, programming and symbolic algebra (CAS) are what separate graphing calculators from scientific ones. Most people wouldn't expect those on a plain scientific calculator.

If this is a spec for something you're building, the first five sections cover what most users reach for. Base-n and bitwise operations are the most useful extras for programmers.
